Procrastination... the act of postponning painful things. Or, is it?

If there's one thing that plagued humans for ages, that thing is procrastination.

Just so we be clear, I'll give a definition so we ain' miscomunicating here. 

For the purposes of this blog post, procrastination is the act of doing anything other than the thing you know you are suposed to be doing. 

The definition may be, kinda broad, but it keeps procrastination as a concept even when you try to delude yourself ("I'm not procrastinating, I'll just check this real quick").

Imagine you need to wash a days old dishes, and sundenly decide to note down a idea you just had. Is that procrastination? Well, I would say yes. Even if that idea will make you a million bucks, you are still postponning something you should be doing. 

That definition also describes the hypocrital nature of procrastionation. It's not just about avoiding the hard. In fact, you may do things that are **harder** just to avoid doing that thing. 

For example, in the cartoon The Amazing World of Gumball, the main characters do increasingly stupid things, instead of just taking out the trash. That seems ridiculous, but for the last few days I have been doing Nand2Tetris course and not doing everything that I was suposed to be doing.

So, if procrastination isn't (just) about avoiding the hard, then what is it? It's about priorities. And ambiguity.

When you start gazing at the sky or read a Warhammer 40k book instead of pick up the clothes to wash, you are procrastinating, our definition agrees with that, but it does not explains why.

Y'see, altough we don't see it, the brain always prioritizes things, pain and benefits. It's why we have a term from getting tired from doing too many decisions (death by a thousand cuts) or why we (aquela palavr que representa a sindrome de ter muitas escolhas). 

If that wasn't the case and we only did what is necessary, certainly dopamine wouldn't have as much of a effect as it has.

Indeed, half the reason we procrastinate is from not knowing what is being prioritized. The other half is knowing what to prioritize.

Even if you say "Here's all the categories which a task should fill", like time to do, difficulty doing and how good is it, what you consider the most important between these? 

Does trhoughput (doing more things in a day) is the most important thing to you? Then doing easy tasks is more important then how much it helps. What if the most important thing is doing big steps? Then how much the tasks helps will be the most important thing.

Another thing that causes procrastionation is ambiguity. I don't know how to describe it, so I'll give you a example. At what moment do you consider a task like "study for the test" done? The moment you open the notebook? The moment close it? After reading 10 pages? When you know what X means? And how do you even "study to the test"?

As another example, say you want to install another operating system in your PC because you use Arch Linux (like I do). At what moment do you consider it "done"? I mean, of course done is "I have the thing in my PC". But what does "doing that" entails? Well, first you have to go to the website, then download, then pass to a pendrive, then reset the computer, do all necessary setup stuff and badabim badabum, it's done.

But say that at that last step you discover you have to reboot the PC to change a small detail (like resize a partition) and redo all that again. Except that you have to install some tool, except the tool isn't working. A small task like "install another OS" becomes a paper weight that stays in your todo list for months after that.

What I'm saying is, sometimes what we call a task is much more then "do the thing". It's easy to wash the dishes. It has a clear done and not done path. Now tell me what is the path to "program a bit of my game dev project" even is.

To conclude this small essay, procrastination is not about the task itself, but how we perceive it.
